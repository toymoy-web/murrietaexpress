Gas Smell In Your Home? Here's Exactly What To Do (And What Not To)

Published September 2026 · Murrieta Express Plumbing

This is a safety situation, not a standard plumbing call. Here's the correct order of steps.

A gas smell in your home is one of the few plumbing-adjacent situations that calls for a genuinely different response than almost anything else in this blog. It's not a "call and schedule a visit" situation. It's a "leave now and call from somewhere safe" situation. Here's the correct sequence, and just as importantly, the things you should specifically avoid doing.

Step One: Leave The Area Immediately

If you smell gas, natural gas is typically described as smelling like rotten eggs due to an additive called mercaptan, get yourself and everyone else out of the house right away. Don't stop to investigate the source or try to find the leak yourself. The priority is getting people away from a potential ignition source, not diagnosing the problem.

Step Two: Don't Operate Any Switches Or Electronics

This is one of the most important and most commonly overlooked instructions. Don't flip light switches, don't use your phone inside the house, don't start your car in an attached garage, and don't operate any appliance. Even a small electrical spark from a switch can be enough to ignite accumulated gas in an enclosed space. Leave switches exactly as they are and get out.

Step Three: Don't Search For The Leak Yourself

It's a natural instinct to want to find the source, but this isn't a DIY situation under any circumstances. Don't use a lighter or match to check for a leak, obviously, but also don't spend time sniffing around appliances or gas lines trying to pinpoint it. That's a job for your gas utility and a licensed plumber, not something to investigate personally while gas may still be actively leaking.

Step Four: Call Your Gas Utility From A Safe Location

Once you're safely away from the house, call your gas utility's emergency line. They can often remotely check for a leak at the meter and will dispatch someone to assess the situation, sometimes shutting off gas service to the property remotely or on arrival if needed. This call should happen before calling a plumber, since the utility company is set up specifically for this kind of emergency response.

Step Five: Call A Licensed Plumber For The Actual Repair

Once the gas utility has addressed the immediate safety concern, whether that's confirming there's no active leak or shutting off service until repairs happen, a licensed plumber handles the actual gas line repair. This is not a repair to attempt yourself or to have handled by anyone without the appropriate license, since gas line work carries real safety stakes and is subject to permitting requirements for good reason.

What Usually Causes A Gas Smell

A few common causes account for most residential gas smell reports. A pilot light that's gone out on an older appliance can release unburned gas into the air. A loose fitting or connection at an appliance, a water heater or range for example, can develop a slow leak over time. Less commonly, a damaged or corroded section of gas piping itself is the source. None of these are things to diagnose yourself in the moment; they're all things a professional identifies after the immediate safety response has already happened.

What If The Smell Is Faint And You're Not Sure?

Treat any detectable gas smell the same way, whether it seems strong or faint. A faint smell can indicate a small leak that's just as real as a strong one, and gas concentration in a space can change quickly depending on airflow and ventilation. There's no version of "it's probably nothing" that changes the correct response here. Leave, don't operate anything electrical, and call your utility.

After The Immediate Emergency Is Resolved

Once the gas utility and a plumber have addressed the actual cause, it's worth asking whether any other gas appliances or connections in the home are due for a check. This matters particularly if the home is older or if this wasn't the first time you've noticed a smell, even briefly. A pattern of recurring smells, even small ones, is worth a more thorough inspection rather than treating each incident as an unrelated, isolated event.

Gas Appliances Worth Knowing About In Your Home

Most homes have more gas connections than people realize: a water heater, a range or cooktop, sometimes a furnace or boiler, and occasionally an outdoor fire feature or barbecue hookup. Each connection point is a potential source, and each ages differently depending on use and exposure to the elements. If you've never had a whole-home gas line inspection and your house is more than a decade or two old, it's a reasonable thing to schedule proactively rather than waiting for a smell to prompt it. That's especially true if you've noticed even a faint odor near any appliance in the past.

Teaching Everyone In The Household What To Do

The steps above only work if everyone in the household knows them, not just whoever happens to be reading this now. Take a few minutes to make sure other adults, and older children capable of understanding the instructions, know that a gas smell means leave immediately, don't touch switches, and call from outside. In an actual emergency, there often isn't time for one person to direct everyone else, so having the response already understood in advance genuinely matters.

Why This Guide Exists Alongside Our Other Plumbing Content

Most of what we write about covers plumbing repairs that, while sometimes urgent, aren't immediate safety emergencies in the way a gas leak is. This one's different, and we've written it as its own standalone reference specifically because the stakes and the correct sequence of steps are genuinely different from a standard plumbing call. If you've read this before ever smelling gas in your own home, that's exactly the right time to have read it. Bookmark it, or better yet, make sure at least one other adult in your household knows where to find it too. Common Questions

What does natural gas actually smell like?

It's typically described as smelling like rotten eggs, due to an additive called mercaptan added specifically so leaks are noticeable, since natural gas itself is odorless.

Should I call 911 or my gas utility first?

Call your gas utility's emergency line first from a safe location outside the home. They're specifically equipped to respond to gas leak reports quickly.

Is it ever okay to try to find the leak myself?

No. Leave the investigation and any repair work to your gas utility and a licensed plumber. This isn't a situation for personal troubleshooting.
