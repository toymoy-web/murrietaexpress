Pool Owners In Murrieta: How To Tell If A Leak Is Your Plumber's Job Or Your Pool Company's

Published September 2026 · Murrieta Express Plumbing

Both trades work on pools, but they cover genuinely different parts of the system.

Pools are common in Murrieta's master-planned neighborhoods, and pool leaks are one of those problems where homeowners often aren't sure who to call first. A plumber and a pool company both work on parts of a pool system, but they cover different territory, and calling the wrong one first can mean a wasted visit and a repeat diagnostic fee. Here's how to actually tell the difference before you pick up the phone.

The Two Basic Categories Of Pool Leak

Pool leaks generally fall into one of two categories. The first is a plumbing-side leak: a problem with the equipment pad connections, the buried supply and return lines running between the pool and the pad, or the autofill valve. The second is a structural leak in the pool shell or liner itself, a crack in the gunite or plaster, or a tear in a vinyl liner. A plumber handles the first category. A pool company handles the second.

Signs That Point Toward The Plumbing Side

A few patterns suggest the leak is more likely on the plumbing side. A wet or soggy area specifically near the equipment pad, rather than near the pool itself, is a strong indicator. Air showing up in the pump basket often points to a suction-side leak in the buried lines. An autofill valve running noticeably more than it used to, without any other obvious explanation, also points toward a plumbing connection rather than the pool structure itself.

Signs That Point Toward The Pool Company

Other patterns point the other direction. If the pool loses water at roughly the same rate whether the pump is running or completely off, that's a sign the water is escaping directly through the shell or liner. Plumbing-side leaks are more directly tied to water actively moving through pipes and fittings, so a leak rate that doesn't change with the pump usually isn't coming from there. Visible cracks in the pool's surface, or a liner that's visibly torn or separated, obviously point toward structural repair rather than plumbing.

What Happens When You're Not Sure

This is exactly the situation a lot of homeowners find themselves in, and it's a completely reasonable reason to call a plumber first. Our pool leak detection process checks the equipment pad and pressure tests the buried lines as a starting point, since that's squarely within a plumber's scope and often the easier, less expensive place to look. If everything checks out on the plumbing side, we'll tell you honestly and point you toward a pool specialist for shell or liner diagnosis, rather than guessing outside our actual expertise.

Why We Don't Try To Do Both

Pool shell and liner leak detection requires specialized equipment and expertise that's genuinely different from standard plumbing diagnostic tools. A pool company that does this work regularly has dye testing kits, and experience reading subtle changes in a pool's structure that a general plumber simply doesn't develop through plumbing work alone. We'd rather refer you to someone who actually specializes in that diagnosis than take a guess and potentially send you toward the wrong fix.

What The Actual Diagnostic Process Looks Like

When we check the plumbing side, we start by visually inspecting all accessible connections at the equipment pad, since a loose fitting or a worn gasket is sometimes visible without any special equipment at all. From there, if nothing is visibly wrong, pressure testing the suction and return lines can confirm or rule out a leak in the buried plumbing without needing to excavate anything. This whole process is often completed in a single visit, giving you a clear answer either way before deciding on next steps.

What This Costs Either Way

An equipment pad and plumbing-line diagnosis is generally more affordable than a full pool leak detection service, since it doesn't require draining the pool or specialized dye testing equipment. If the issue turns out to be plumbing-side, repair costs vary based on what's actually found, but they're typically less than shell or liner repair. If the issue turns out to be structural, you'll have ruled out the plumbing side first, which means the pool company you call next can focus their diagnostic effort more efficiently rather than starting from scratch.

A Practical Starting Point

If your pool is losing water and you're not sure where to start, checking the equipment pad area yourself for any visible wetness is a reasonable first step before calling anyone. Beyond that, calling a plumber first makes sense in most cases, since ruling out the plumbing side is usually faster and less expensive. A plumber who does this regularly can tell you honestly and quickly whether you need to make a second call to a pool specialist.

What To Mention When You Call

When you call to schedule, mention a few specific details that help speed up the diagnosis. Tell us roughly how much water the pool is losing and how quickly, and whether you've noticed the loss with the pump running versus off. Also mention whether there's any visible wetness anywhere around the equipment pad or yard. These details help a technician arrive prepared with the right expectations rather than starting completely from scratch on site.

Common Equipment Pad Issues We Actually Find

A few specific problems show up repeatedly at equipment pads. Union fittings, the threaded connections that allow pump and filter components to be removed for service, are a common source of slow leaks as their O-rings age and compress over years of use. Pressure gauge fittings and the small ports feeding chemical feeders or automation systems are another frequent culprit, since these smaller connections see less attention during routine pool maintenance than the major components do. Both are usually straightforward, affordable fixes once actually identified. The same goes for a nearby hose bib feeding the pad's fill line, which is worth checking before assuming the leak is inside the equipment itself.

If The Diagnosis Points To The Pool Company Instead

If our plumbing-side check comes back clean and the pattern of water loss points toward the shell or liner, we'll tell you that directly rather than continuing to look for a plumbing explanation that isn't there. At that point, a pool company's dye testing and structural inspection is the appropriate next step, and having already ruled out the plumbing side means they can focus their diagnostic effort more efficiently rather than needing to also check equipment pad connections themselves.

Common Questions

Can a plumber fix a crack in my pool's plaster or gunite?

No, that's structural pool repair work outside a general plumber's scope. We'll refer you to a pool specialist if the diagnosis points that direction.

Is it more expensive to call both a plumber and a pool company?

It can mean two diagnostic visits if the first doesn't identify the cause, but checking the plumbing side first is usually the more affordable starting point given the equipment involved.

Do you drain the pool to check for a plumbing leak?

Usually not. Equipment pad inspection and pressure testing the buried lines can typically be done without draining the pool.
