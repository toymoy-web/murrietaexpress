Water Softener Or Water Filter? What Murrieta Homeowners Keep Mixing Up

Published September 2026 · Murrieta Express Plumbing

They get lumped together constantly, but they solve two genuinely different problems.

Of all the questions we get about water treatment in Murrieta, this is probably the most common one. Someone notices scale on their fixtures or a strange taste in their water, and they're not sure whether the fix is a softener, a filter, or both. The confusion is understandable. Both systems get installed near where water enters the home, both are marketed under the broad umbrella of "water treatment," and salespeople don't always take the time to explain the actual difference. Here's the plain version.

What A Water Softener Actually Does

A softener removes hardness minerals, mainly calcium and magnesium, through a process called ion exchange. Water passes through a tank filled with resin beads that swap out those hardness minerals for sodium or potassium ions. The result is water that no longer leaves scale behind on fixtures, inside water heaters, or on dishes and glassware. Softening is specifically about mineral content. It doesn't touch chlorine taste, odor, or sediment.

What A Water Filter Actually Does

A filter addresses a completely different set of issues: taste, odor, chlorine, and sediment. Depending on the type, a filter might use activated carbon to remove chlorine taste and smell, a sediment cartridge to catch fine particles, or a combination of stages for more thorough treatment. A filter does not remove hardness minerals. Water that passes through a filter but not a softener will still leave the same scale behind on your fixtures and inside your water heater.

Why Murrieta Homes Usually Need One More Than The Other

Murrieta's water commonly falls in the 10 to 25 grains per gallon hardness range, which is genuinely hard by Southern California standards. For most households here, that hardness is the bigger practical problem. It's what's shortening your water heater's life, coating your fixtures, and making it harder to work up a lather in the shower. A filter won't touch any of that. If your main complaint is equipment protection and reduced scale buildup, a softener is the piece that actually does that job.

When A Filter Makes More Sense

If your actual complaint is taste or smell rather than scale, a filter is the more direct fix. Chlorine taste is common on treated municipal water, and a decent carbon filter addresses it well. Sediment, occasionally an issue after nearby water main work, is also a filter's job, not a softener's. Some households genuinely need both: a softener for equipment protection and scale prevention, and a filter for taste and odor on top of that.

Can You Just Get One System That Does Both?

Some combination systems exist that pair softening resin with a carbon filtration stage in one unit. These can be a reasonable option if you know upfront you want both functions. A standalone softener plus a standalone under-sink filter for drinking water specifically sometimes gives more flexibility, though, since you can service or replace each component independently rather than dealing with a single combined unit.

How To Actually Figure Out What You Need

The most reliable way to know is a water test specific to your address, since hardness and other water quality factors can vary somewhat by neighborhood and by whether a property is on well water. A test tells you your actual hardness number and flags anything else worth addressing, rather than guessing based on a citywide average or a generic sales pitch. Most estimates for either system include this test as a standard first step, not an upsell.

A Quick Way To Tell Which One You Actually Need

If you're trying to figure this out on your own before calling anyone, ask yourself what's actually bothering you. White, chalky buildup around faucets and showerheads points toward hardness, meaning a softener is the fix. A chlorine smell or taste straight from the tap points toward filtration instead. An early water heater failure, more than one repair well before the 8 to 10 year mark, is a strong sign hardness is the underlying issue even if nobody explicitly diagnosed it that way at the time. If you're dealing with more than one of these symptoms at once, that's usually a sign both systems would genuinely help rather than just one.

The Bottom Line

Softening and filtration aren't competing options, they're answers to different questions. Murrieta's hard water makes softening the higher-priority fix for most households here, since it's what's actually protecting your plumbing and appliances from ongoing damage. Filtration is a worthwhile addition if taste or odor is also a real complaint, but it's not a substitute for addressing hardness if that's the underlying issue driving repeat water heater repairs or visible scale buildup.

What Happens If You Only Install One And Later Need The Other

Adding the second system later isn't a major project if you start with just one. A whole-home softener and an under-sink filter operate independently, so installing a softener now and adding filtration for drinking water later, or the reverse, doesn't require redoing the first installation. The main practical consideration is where each unit needs to sit relative to your main water line and any existing plumbing, which a technician can map out during either installation so a future addition stays straightforward rather than requiring rework.

What Renters And New Homeowners Should Check First

If you've just moved into a Murrieta home and aren't sure whether it already has a softener, a filter, or neither, start with a quick visual check near the main water line, often in the garage or a utility closet. That usually reveals an existing system if one's installed. If you find a softener already in place, it's worth confirming when it was last serviced, since a resin tank that hasn't been maintained can lose effectiveness over time even though it's still physically connected and running. A technician can test whether an existing system is actually still softening effectively or just sitting there without doing its job anymore.

What A Reasonable Estimate Actually Includes

A proper estimate for either system should start with a water test, not a sales pitch based on a generic citywide number. From there, the specifics that actually affect price include your household's water usage, which determines system capacity, and whether existing plumbing near the main line makes installation straightforward or requires some rerouting. A technician should be able to explain clearly why a particular capacity or configuration fits your specific home, rather than defaulting to a one-size-fits-all recommendation regardless of household size.

Common Questions

Will a water softener make my drinking water taste different?

Softening itself doesn't significantly change taste, though some people notice a slightly different feel due to the sodium exchange. If taste is a concern, a filter addresses that separately.

Do I need a plumber to install either system?

Yes, for a proper whole-home installation connected at the main line. A basic under-sink filter is sometimes DIY-friendly, but a softener installation is best handled professionally to ensure correct sizing and connection.

How do I know my exact water hardness number?

A water test specific to your address is the most reliable way, since it can vary by neighborhood and water source. Most installation estimates include this test.
